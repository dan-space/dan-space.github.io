MY EXTENSIONS / SYNTHESIS

Ideas I keep coming back to.

01

Capability ≠ Priority

Being able to build something does not automatically make it the most important thing to build.

02

Knowledge is Situated

What we know depends partly on how we observe, measure, model, intervene, and define the problem.

03

Build with Humility

Complex technologies should be designed with explicit awareness of uncertainty, failure, and limits of prediction.

04

Every Technology Rewrites a System

A new device can alter behavior, power, institutions, and relationships far beyond its intended technical function.

05

Values Enter Through Design

Data selection, rules, optimization targets, interfaces, and access all embed judgments about what matters.

06

Governance is Part of Engineering

When systems affect many people, feedback from users, communities, experts, institutions, and policymakers becomes part of responsible design.

07

The Future is a Design Responsibility

Technology expands what is possible; human beings still have to decide which possibilities become the world we live in.

FROM MY JOHN LOCKE ESSAY

A debate about space is really a debate about priorities.

NECESSITYRESOURCESHUMAN NEEDSPRIORITY

Space exploration can create knowledge and new technology, but limited resources force societies to choose. If hunger, disease, and poverty remain unresolved, technological ambition cannot be justified by possibility alone. The harder question is not whether humanity can go farther, but what deserves to come first.

A QUESTION I KEEPWhen resources are limited, how should a civilization decide what counts as progress?
MY ESSAY / JOHN LOCKE

Progress requires priorities.

My essay on space exploration moved from “Can we?” to “Should this come first?” It made technological progress a problem of allocation, justice, and human need rather than capability alone.

PROF. LIU PENG / PHILOSOPHY OF SCIENTIFIC PRACTICE

Science does not merely describe the world.

Scientific knowledge emerges through experiments, instruments, models, materials, institutions, and intervention. Practice does not simply test finished ideas; it helps make scientific objects and knowledge possible.

PROF. LIU PENG / ROBOTS IN HUMAN SOCIETY

Technology enters society as a relationship.

A robot is not merely a new object placed into an old world. Its arrival changes roles, rules, responsibilities, expectations, and even the people who live with it. Integration therefore requires continuous social reconstruction.

THE HUMAN QUESTION

The more I learned how to build systems, the more I became interested in the people who have to live inside them. A technically optimal solution is not always a humanly desirable one. A city can maximize efficiency and still feel isolating; an algorithm can optimize performance while increasing anxiety; a robot can replace dangerous work while changing dignity, responsibility, and human relationships. Technology does not simply solve problems—it also changes the people and societies that use it.

A WORKING PHILOSOPHY

Science gives us new ways to understand and intervene in the world. Technology turns those possibilities into power. Philosophy asks us to choose a direction.
